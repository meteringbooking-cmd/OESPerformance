Rumours swirl that TOM302 Newcastle to Enfidha flight may be missing its left flange

Experts are unable to confirm or deny the whereabouts of the left flange on TOMjet flight TOM302, which departed Newcastle International Airport earlier today bound for Enfidha-Hammamet International Airport, Tunisia.

By Alistair Pennywhistle, BBC Aviation Correspondent  |  Published: Today, 09:47  |  Updated: 11 minutes ago

Unconfirmed reports emerging from aviation circles this morning suggest that TOMjet flight TOM302, currently somewhere over the English Channel or possibly France, may be operating with an absent, misplaced, or otherwise unaccounted-for left flange.

The aircraft, believed to be carrying holidaymakers in various states of pre-holiday excitement, departed Newcastle International at its scheduled time. Ground crew reportedly noticed something "a bit off" about the left side of the aircraft, though witnesses disagree on what, exactly, constitutes a flange and whether the one in question was ever there to begin with.

"I'm not saying it definitely doesn't have a left flange. I'm also not saying it does. What I am saying is that in my 34 years of standing next to aircraft, I have never seen a flange quite so absent."

— Derek Partridge, Senior Apron Marshal, Newcastle International Airport

Passengers on board TOM302 are understood to be largely unaware of the flange situation, most being occupied with the in-flight entertainment, a Boots meal deal, or trying to recline their seats 4 millimetres into the knees of the passenger behind them.

What we know so far

  • TOM302 departed Newcastle International Airport this morning bound for Enfidha-Hammamet International, Tunisia
  • Rumours of a missing left flange emerged approximately 23 minutes after takeoff
  • The right flange has been visually confirmed and is reported to be "doing great"
  • TOMjet has declined to comment on the precise location of the left flange
  • Aviation experts confirm flanges are, technically, a real thing
  • No flanges have been found in the departures lounge at time of publication

A spokesperson for TOMjet told the BBC: "We can confirm that our aircraft are maintained to the highest standards, that all required components are in their designated positions, and that we will not be issuing a statement specifically about flanges at this time." The spokesperson then hung up immediately.

The Civil Aviation Authority said it was "monitoring the situation closely," which aviation reporters interpret as meaning someone has written "FLANGE?" on a Post-it note and stuck it to a monitor.

Expert Reaction

👨‍✈️

"A flange, in the broader sense, serves an important flanging function. Whether this particular flange is load-bearing is a question above my pay grade. Or anyone's, frankly."

Prof. Gerald Mumford, Chair of Aeronautical Ambiguity, Cranfield University

Members of the public have taken to social media with wildly varying responses. One user wrote "thoughts and prayers for the flange," while another — identifying themselves as an aerospace engineer — posted a 47-slide thread explaining why flanges are fine, before deleting it without explanation.

Flight tracking websites show TOM302 proceeding normally along its planned route, which aviation analysts note is "exactly what a plane with a missing flange would do, if it was fine, which it probably is."

The BBC will continue to provide updates on this developing flange situation. Passengers due to fly TOM302 on the return leg are advised to check the TOMjet website, where no information about flanges is currently available.
